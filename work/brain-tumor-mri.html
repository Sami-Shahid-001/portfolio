Brain Tumour MRI Classification

Two models and one honest lesson. A custom CNN looked excellent on validation and generalised poorly; transfer learning closed part of that gap, from 63% to 74% on MRI scans neither model had seen.

01

The problem

Classify a brain MRI scan into one of four classes: glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumour, or no tumour. Medical imaging datasets are small and uneven, which makes it easy to build a model that scores well on its own validation split and badly on anything else.

GliomaMeningiomaPituitaryNo tumour

So the goal was never just accuracy. It was accuracy on scans the model had genuinely never seen, and an honest account of where it fails.

02

A custom CNN first

I started from scratch to understand what a CNN actually learns. Images were resized to 128×128, converted to RGB and normalised to the 0 to 1 range; the network grew to six convolution blocks with max pooling, global average pooling instead of flattening, a dense layer with 0.3 dropout and a four-way softmax, about 247,000 trainable parameters.

One detail is worth admitting: the network started with three blocks, and re-running notebook cells duplicated layers in memory, quietly producing a deeper model. Noticing that, and deciding deliberately what the architecture should be, was its own lesson.

03

Where it broke

92 to 93%Training accuracy
89 to 90%Validation accuracy
63%Unseen test accuracy
GliomaHardest class

Validation accuracy said the model was nearly done. Unseen scans said otherwise: performance fell to about 63%, with glioma confused most often, its appearance overlaps with the other tumour types more than theirs overlap with each other.

04

Transfer learning

Instead of learning edges, textures and shapes from a few thousand scans, the second model starts from a convolutional backbone pretrained on ImageNet and uses it as a feature extractor. Global average pooling, a dense layer, dropout and a softmax sit on top; then selected upper layers of the backbone are unfrozen and fine-tuned on MRI data.

05

Results

ModelTrainingValidationUnseen test
Custom CNN~92 to 93%~89 to 90%~63%
Transfer learning~94 to 96%~90%~74%

The validation numbers are almost identical. The difference only appears on unseen data, which is exactly why it had to be measured there. An eleven-point improvement came from better features, not a better-looking validation curve.

06

Deployment

Both models were deployed as Streamlit apps: upload a scan, get class probabilities back in real time. Testing in the app on real images exposed weaknesses that never showed up in the notebook, which is the main reason to deploy a learning project at all.

07

What holds, and what does not

What holds: the improvement on unseen data is real and reproducible on that test set, and the comparison is like-for-like.

What does not: 74% is not clinical-grade, glioma is still the weakest class, and the next steps are clear, a larger and more varied dataset, augmentation, hyperparameter search and saliency maps that show what the model is looking at. This is a learning project, not a diagnostic tool.
